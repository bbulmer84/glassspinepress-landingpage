Sample — Opening Chapter

Baseline Reality

Baseline Reality

The building had been renamed twice in the last decade, but the smell never changed.

Antiseptic. Old carpet glue. Burnt coffee that never quite left the vents.

The building had the anonymous confidence of places designed to outlast their purpose—glass, concrete, and soft edges meant to reassure. It wasn’t quite a hospital and not quite a university; its corridors carried the language of care and research in equal measure, as if no one had ever bothered to decide which mattered more.

Elias checked in with the same badge he’d used since the year his son started middle school—an unremarkable rectangle of plastic with a grainy photo that had become steadily less accurate.

He caught his reflection briefly in the elevator’s brushed steel—dark hair gone greyer than he remembered, thinning at the temples, glasses he kept meaning to replace sitting slightly crooked on his nose. He straightened his jacket, anyway, smoothing a crease that hadn’t mattered five years ago. The gesture felt automatic now—less vanity than camouflage. He looked like what he was: a man who slept enough to function but not enough to feel rested, dressed in clothes chosen for durability rather than impression.

The turnstile blinked green. The lobby screens rotated through the usual loop: donation milestones, research “wins,” a smiling couple in matching hospital gowns holding up a clipboard like it was a trophy.

The space itself was arranged like a promise—bright, open, deliberately calm. Every display translated complexity into reassurance: charts smoothed into upward lines, outcomes framed as stories, numbers rounded until they felt humane. Finished things didn’t invite questions. They invited trust. Nothing here felt experimental. Everything felt finished.

A nurse at the information desk lifted her chin in greeting without looking up from her tablet. Elias returned it, not because he expected her to notice, but because that’s what you did in places like this. Polite motions. Soft edges. Everyone moving like they belonged.

He waited for the elevator with three other people who were all pretending not to be listening to the same voice.

A ceiling speaker, tuned just low enough to be ignored, delivered the morning’s “facility announcements” in an even tone: parking updates, construction detours, reminders about “communication best practices,” a note that the cafeteria was trialling a new menu layout “to reduce decision fatigue.”

The voice never rushed and never paused for emphasis, as if it had been designed to sound considerate rather than informative.

Decision fatigue. The phrase had appeared everywhere lately, as if the world had discovered a new sin.

Elias stepped into the elevator, pressed four, and watched the door close on the lobby’s clean optimism.

He was late by three minutes.

Not late in any meaningful sense. Nobody would call him. Nobody would write him up. The meeting would start when it started, the same way meetings always did—waiting politely for someone important to arrive and then pretending that waiting hadn’t happened.

Still, the fact of the three minutes sat in his mind like a pebble under the tongue. Small. Irritating. Hard to stop noticing once it was there.

On the fourth floor, the hallway light shifted subtly as he walked—adaptive LEDs responding to motion, brightness, time of day, occupancy. The building felt like it was breathing around him, adjusting itself without ever asking permission. He’d read a paper once that argued people were calmer when environments “responded.” The data was decent. The conclusion had been too clean.

A wall-mounted display outside the conference room showed a rotating slate of “engagement metrics” for the floor: average meeting duration, email response time, “collaboration density,” and—new this month—something called Friction Index.

None of the metrics explained how they were calculated. They didn’t need to. He wondered briefly who the display was for—then realised the question itself was out of date. The numbers existed to be seen, not questioned.

Elias paused long enough to read it properly.

FRICTION INDEX: LOW (DESIRABLE)

CORRECTION LATENCY: MODERATE

TONE CONSISTENCY: HIGH

The parenthetical bothered Elias more than the numbers. It didn’t describe the world. It told you how to feel about it.

He frowned, not because the words frightened him, but because he couldn’t quite place where he’d started seeing them first. Work. Feeds. School newsletters. Airline updates. The language was creeping outward from somewhere, colonising ordinary life one bland phrase at a time.

Elias couldn’t remember when he’d first started noticing it—only that it had become harder to think without borrowing its terms.

Inside the room, the conference space was too polished for disagreement—glass table, soft chairs, sound-dampening panels that swallowed raised voices before they could exist. It was a room designed for alignment, not discovery.

Dr. Helen Ward was already seated, a paper notebook open in front of her like a small act of defiance. A faint ink smudge marked the side of her index finger, stubborn as muscle memory. Her jacket was folded over the back of the chair, sleeves rolled just past the wrist—a habit she’d never broken from lab work. Her hair was pulled back tightly enough to suggest control rather than style.

She didn’t look up until he took the chair beside her.

“You’re late,” she said, barely moving her mouth.

“Three minutes.”

“Still late.”

He gave her a look that meant I’m allowed to be human, and she gave him one back that meant not in this building you’re not.

Across the table, Thomas Hale stood with his hands clasped in front of him, posture perfect, suit that fit too well to have been chosen for comfort. The dark fabric showed no lint, no wrinkle; even his visitor badge was clipped with deliberate precision. He looked ageless in the way policy men often did—maintained rather than lived in.

He wasn’t hospital staff. He didn’t pretend to be. He had a visitor badge clipped to his lapel like it was a medal.

Hale was already speaking, voice smooth and careful, as if every sentence had passed through a filter before it left his mouth.

“—and so, the objective is alignment,” Hale said, smiling gently at the group as if the word meant compromise. Elias noticed how carefully the term avoided sharper alternatives—agreement, enforcement, compliance—without actually excluding them.

Elias felt the familiar pressure to nod—to keep pace with the room even when his feet hadn’t moved. He’d heard the phrasing before: not agreement, alignment. Nobody needed to love the direction. They only needed to move in it.

Elias had heard that one before, too. On a panel. In a briefing. In a corporate ad that had been disguised as a public service announcement.

He listened without interrupting, because interrupting never helped. It only marked you as a problem to be managed.

On the screen at the front of the room, a slide displayed three columns: EFFICIENCY, STABILITY, TRUST. Under each was a neat list of bullet points. The bullets were all verbs: streamline, reduce, reinforce, dampen, elevate.

The slide would be gone by tomorrow. The language would not. There were no nouns that could be argued with, no claims that could be tested—just movement, presented as inevitability.

“We’re implementing a standardised communications protocol across partner institutions,” Hale continued. “Healthcare. Education. Media. Faith-based outreach. The goal is coherence in times of elevated social temperature.”

Elias had heard worse phrases dressed up more honestly. This one treated people like weather—unpredictable, measurable, and ultimately manageable.

Elias’s eyes snagged on that phrase: elevated social temperature.

Someone had decided the world was running a fever. Someone within the system had decided the fever was the problem.

A woman from administration—Marcy, he thought, or maybe Macy—raised her hand slightly. Not all the way. Not enough to stop the flow. Just enough to signal submission before speaking.

“How will this impact research publication cycles?”

Hale’s smile didn’t change. “It won’t. Not in content. Only in presentation. We want to make sure our messaging supports community stability.”

Presentation. Messaging. Stability.

Elias looked down at his own hands. They were clean. Pale under the fluorescent light. He imagined them as they’d been when he was younger, stained with marker ink from lab labels, a small cut across the knuckle from rushing. He used to like the messy part. The part where work showed.

Now everything was smooth. Sanitised. The building smelt like it always had, but the world felt increasingly like it was being wiped down.

Helen’s pen moved across her paper notebook. Elias leaned just enough to see what she’d written.

Coherence = control.

Stability > accuracy?

He exhaled through his nose. She’d written his thought before he’d properly formed it.

Hale clicked to the next slide. A simple diagram: a circle of arrows feeding into itself. Inputs becoming outputs becoming inputs again. A feedback loop, drawn like it was a natural cycle. The arrows erased beginnings. Endpoints implied responsibility. It looked less like a system someone had built and more like something that simply happened, as if no one were responsible for its direction.

“This,” Hale said, tapping the diagram, “is what we’re addressing. Amplification. The system doesn’t self-correct the way it used to,” he said. “People don’t encounter friction naturally anymore. So, we introduce friction deliberately—at the right points—so we keep the overall trajectory stable.”

Elias shifted in his chair.

“What points?” he asked, keeping his voice neutral.

Hale’s gaze found him. It wasn’t hostile. It wasn’t warm. It was calibrated.

“Wherever the cascade begins,” Hale said. “Content moderation. Recommendation weighting. Credential reinforcement. Community leaders who can reframe uncertainty as trust.”

Elias could have argued the premise. He could have asked what “trajectory” meant and who got to define it. He could have asked how introducing friction “deliberately” wasn’t simply another word for manipulation.

But he already knew the answers he’d get: We’re all in this together. It’s for safety. It’s temporary. It’s evidence based. The phrases were stacked and ready like sandbags.

So, he asked something he could measure.

“What’s the metric for success?”

Hale’s smile widened, as if grateful for the question. “Reduced volatility,” he said. “Higher engagement with verified sources. Reduced spread of destabilising narratives. Increased trust in institutions.”

The word landed with the weight of something that had once been earned.

“Trust,” Elias repeated.

“Yes,” Hale said softly, like he was saying something sacred.

Elias didn’t trust institutions because they were institutions. He trusted processes when they admitted their limits. He trusted people when they changed their mind in the face of evidence. He trusted systems that could be corrected.

He wanted to say that.

Instead, he nodded once, the way you nod when the world is moving, and you don’t want it to mark you as stationary.

The meeting continued.

They discussed implementation timelines. A new internal portal. A “tone guide.” Pre-approved language for sensitive topics. A shared repository of “trusted framings” that partner organisations could adapt.

Trusted framings.

Elias wrote the phrase in the margin of his own notes and felt a thin strand of irritation tighten behind his eyes. He wasn’t naive. He’d lived through enough to know that language mattered, that framing shaped perception. He’d taught undergraduates the difference between correlation and causation and watched their faces light up when they realised how easily a sentence could lie without ever using a false number.

But this was different.

This wasn’t teaching people to think.

This was teaching people to feel safe.

And safety, Elias had learned, was the most persuasive thing in the world.

When the meeting ended, Hale shook hands with everyone, each handshake identical in pressure and duration. When he reached Elias, he held on a fraction of a second longer than necessary.

“We value your work,” Hale said.

Elias could hear the unspoken second sentence: Don’t make it harder than it needs to be.

“I value accuracy,” Elias replied, and kept his tone polite enough that it could be mistaken for agreement.

Hale’s smile didn’t flicker. “Of course,” he said. The sentence closed the discussion the way a lid closed a container—cleanly, firmly, without asking what was inside. “Accuracy supports stability.”

Helen waited until Hale had left the room before speaking.

“Did he just… did he just say that like it was a given?” she asked.

Elias gathered his papers slowly, buying himself a breath. “He said it like it was a slogan,” he answered.

Helen shut her notebook. “Do you remember when people used to argue about facts?”

“They still do,” Elias said.

“No, I mean…” She searched for the words, and for a moment her composure broke, revealing something tired underneath. “Do you remember when facts had… weight? When they weren’t just another input?”

Elias didn’t answer immediately. He glanced at the wall display outside the room again. Friction Index. Tone Consistency.

Correction Latency: Moderate.

“People still care,” he said finally, and hated how much it sounded like reassurance.

Helen studied him. She knew him well enough not to take comfort from his tone.

“Lunch?” she asked, as if food could make the air normal again.

“I’ve got a lab review,” Elias said. “Then I need to get home. Mara’s doing that thing tonight.”

Helen smiled faintly. “The Thursday thing.”

“The Thursday thing,” Elias confirmed.

He left the conference area and walked the corridor toward his office, passing glass-walled rooms where people sat in soft chairs with tablets on their knees, heads bent together like prayer. The corridor lights brightened ahead of him and dimmed behind, shepherding motion without instruction.

There were posters everywhere—bright, friendly fonts urging mindfulness, encouraging community, reminding staff to “model calm.”

Model calm.

The phrase made him want to laugh, which was a dangerous impulse in a hallway full of people who already looked like they were performing.

His office was small, cramped with books that no one ever borrowed anymore. He kept them anyway. Paper was honest. It could still be wrong—but it failed loudly, where anyone could see. Paper didn’t adjust itself to improve your “experience.” Paper didn’t quietly reorder your priorities based on an unseen scoring system.

Elias worked in the unglamorous middle of neuroscience, where progress came in fractions and nobody expected miracles: long-term signal degradation, stress response, the quiet failures that accumulated without ever making headlines.

He set his bag down and woke his computer. The screen lit with a dashboard he hadn’t chosen—an institutional start page that always loaded first now, despite his settings. A banner across the top announced:

COMMUNITY COHERENCE UPDATE

New tone guide available. Review and acknowledge by end of day.

Elias clicked away from it and opened his lab review notes. His own project sat in the quiet middle of his life: tedious, incremental work on neural signal degradation in long-term patients. Not world changing. Useful. Real. The kind of research you did because it mattered to one person at a time, not because it made a headline.

Most days were meant to be spent one floor down, where the air smelled faintly of steriliser and circuitry and the problems were small enough to be honest. He missed that today.

He liked that about it.

He worked for an hour, then another. Emails came in. Most were ordinary: scheduling, grant paperwork, a reminder about compliance training. One was from Helen.

Subject: Tone guide

Body: Did you read it yet? There’s a section on “correction language.” It’s… weird.

Elias stared at the email a moment too long.

Correction language.

He clicked the coherence banner despite himself. A PDF opened—white pages, pastel headers, cheerful icons. The tone guide read like something meant for kindergarten teachers and hostage negotiators at the same time. Every sentence was gentle. None of them were optional.

There was a section titled:

Responding to Misinformation Without Amplifying It

It advised avoiding direct negations (“That is false”) and instead using “affirmative reframes” (“Here’s what we know”). It suggested acknowledging “underlying emotions” before addressing claims. It warned against “corrective confrontations” that could “increase resistance.”

Elias recognised the research behind it—and the convenience. You could correct someone without ever admitting they’d been wrong.

Elias read it once, then read it again slower.

The words weren’t entirely wrong. That was the most dangerous part. He could see the psychology. He understood the research on backfire effects and identity-protective cognition. He’d lectured on it.

But he also knew how easily this could be used.

Not to help people understand.

To help people comply.

A small notification slid in from the corner of his screen:

WELLNESS REMINDER: You have been focused for 112 minutes. Consider a short break.

It didn’t ask how he felt. It assumed it knew.

He closed it.

The next email came from an address he didn’t recognise, but the signature block made it clear: a communications liaison, one of the new roles that had appeared like mushrooms after rain.

Subject: Minor wording adjustment (requested)

Body: Hi Dr. Corvanes,

We’re excited to include your lab’s recent findings in next week’s internal spotlight. For tone alignment, can we adjust the line “signal decay increases under stress conditions” to “signal responsiveness changes under high-demand conditions”?

Thanks!—J. Lin, Communications

Elias stared at the sentence.

Signal decay increases under stress conditions.

Signal responsiveness changes under high-demand conditions.

The second sentence wasn’t inaccurate. It simply stepped sideways from responsibility. It was just carefully uninterested in causes.

One was descriptive. One was… polite.

He rolled his chair back, the wheels whispering against the old carpet. His office window looked out at another building wall. There was no view, just glass and concrete and the faint reflection of himself: a man in his late forties who still believed that if you measured carefully enough, reality would behave.

He picked up his phone and checked the time. Mara would be done at the community centre soon. Their son would be home from school already, probably in his room with headphones on, trying to be invisible in the way teenagers did when they couldn’t decide whether they wanted to be seen.

Elias typed a reply to J. Lin.

No. “Signal decay” is accurate. “Stress” is the variable. We can add context, but we can’t change the meaning.

He hovered over send for a second longer than necessary, feeling the familiar tension between being right and being easy.

He pressed send.

Nothing dramatic happened. No alarms. No angry response. No retaliation.

Just a quiet acknowledgement:

Message sent.

Elias felt no satisfaction. Only the faint sense of having placed a small stone on a track that was already moving.

He gathered his things, locked his office, and walked out.

The building’s lobby was busier now. People moved in small currents, badges swinging, conversations kept low. A group of volunteers in matching shirts stood beside a banner that read COMMUNITY UNITY WEEK in bright letters. They handed out pamphlets and stickers with a sunburst logo.

Unity.

Another word that had started showing up everywhere.

Outside, the city was ordinary: traffic, winter-grey sky, the hum of construction somewhere distant. Elias drove home through streets lined with fast-food signs and storefront churches and billboards that changed their message every few seconds, cycling through offers and slogans that all sounded faintly like each other.

At a red light, he glanced at a billboard long enough to catch one phrase before it changed:

TRUST EACH OTHER. TRUST THE PROCESS.

The repetition did the work the sentence refused to do.

The sentence offered reassurance without specifying who “the process” belonged to.

The light turned green.

He drove on.

Home was a modest house in a neighbourhood that had once felt like a safe bet. The place had a little automation—lights on timers, notifications muted at night—but none of the prompts, dashboards, or gentle suggestions about how to feel. Just rooms where things mostly stayed where you left them. Not rich, not struggling—just steady. The kind of place where kids rode bikes in summer and people left their bins out too long on pickup day.

Elias parked in the driveway and sat for a moment with his hands on the steering wheel, letting the quiet of the car settle around him. He waited until the tightness in his chest softened enough to pass for normal, then opened the door.

The day hadn’t been bad. Nothing had happened that should have stayed with him.

And yet there was a weight on his chest like he’d spent the day breathing air that was slightly too thin.

He went inside.

The house smelled like lemon dish soap and whatever candle Mara had decided was “clean” this week. Soft music played from a speaker in the kitchen—something instrumental, gentle enough to be ignored, set to fade out on its own after dinner.

Mara stood at the counter, sleeves rolled up, hair pinned back carelessly, a few strands already slipping loose. She looked exactly as she always did after a long day—comfortable in the space, unguarded, real in a way Elias hadn’t learned to be at work anymore.

She looked up when he walked in, her face brightening in a way that always felt like a small miracle to him, even after years.

“Hey,” she said. “You’re home on time.”

“Mostly,” Elias replied, setting his bag down by the door. He leaned in to kiss her cheek. She smelled like citrus and warm skin.

“How was your day?” she asked, already reaching for the chopping board again.

He considered telling her about Hale. About the tone guide. About the email asking him to soften the truth into something more digestible.

Instead, he said the truest small thing.

“Tiring,” he admitted.

Mara nodded like she’d expected that. “That’s why tonight is low stakes,” she said. “No doom scrolling. No news. Just… us.”

It was the only rule Elias encountered all day that didn’t pretend to be optional.

“The Thursday thing,” Elias said, and she smiled.

“The Thursday thing,” she echoed, as if naming it made it real and protected.

From down the hall, a door clicked—soft, familiar. Their son appeared briefly in the doorway of the kitchen, all awkward length and half-formed confidence, hair falling into his eyes like he hadn’t yet decided whether he wanted to be seen or not, his school jacket hung off one shoulder, badge clipped crookedly where he hadn’t bothered to fix it yet.

He carried himself with the careful quiet of someone who knew how easily attention could turn into expectation.

He hovered like he might leave again if anyone looked at him too directly.

“Hey,” Elias said, gentle.

“Hey,” the boy replied, eyes flicking up for a second before dropping again. He moved toward the fridge, opened it, stared inside like answers might be hidden behind the milk.

Mara watched him with that steady warmth of hers, a warmth that didn’t demand anything. Elias loved her for it, even as he worried that warmth was becoming a kind of liability in a world that rewarded sharper edges.

“Dinner in twenty,” Mara said to their son.

He nodded and retreated, fridge door closing softly behind him.

Elias leaned against the counter and watched Mara work. Her hands were efficient, confident. Ordinary. Safe.

He pulled his phone out of habit, thumb already moving, and stopped himself.

Mara noticed. “You promised,” she said, not scolding—just reminding.

He slid the phone into his pocket.

“Right,” he said. “No screens.”

Mara’s smile softened. “Good.”

For a moment, the house felt like its own sealed little world—an island of routine, smells, small sounds. The music. The knife against the board. The distant thump of something from their son’s room.

Elias let himself believe, just for a breath, that processes still worked. That if something went wrong, someone would correct it. That the world still had friction in the right places.

Mara nudged a bowl toward him. “Taste,” she said.

Elias dipped a spoon, tried it, and nodded. “Good,” he said.

Mara grinned like she’d won something.

And Elias stood in his kitchen, tasting something warm and ordinary, while outside the world kept quietly rewording itself. Rewording, Elias knew now, was how things learned to move without resistance.
