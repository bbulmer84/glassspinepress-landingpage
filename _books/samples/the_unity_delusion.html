Sample — Opening Chapter

Bread and Glass

The market at Dovai was alive that morning, as if sound and colour alone could hold back the grey world pressing in from every side. Awnings stitched from scavenged cloth made a patchwork canopy over the square—rusted reds, sky-bitten blues, yellows faded to straw. Each length of fabric had history in its seams: a curtain from a hotel left to rot, a sail cut from a truck tarp, a ceremonial banner someone had rescued from a pile of cinders. They flapped together and made shade that felt like generosity.

For a heartbeat the square breathed as one before memory of the Order’s silence pressed against its edges.

In the Order’s broadcasts, markets looked colour-graded by function, motion rehearsed until it became grace. Every jar, every fruit, every gesture aligned to purpose. A civic voice whispered of harmony and contribution. That was how she learned obedience could be beautiful. Here, colour still bargained—alive, unapproved, free.

Smells braided into one another and rose warm from the stone. Lentils simmered with cumin in dented pots; rounds of dough blistered and blackened on flat stones; goat fat hissed over coals. Somewhere a pan of onions went too far and sweetened into a dark, smoky perfume. You could taste salt on the air, and dust, and the faint metallic tang of Tomas the tinsmith’s corner where sparks popped and skittered on the ground.

Kara moved through it with her basket hooked tight over her arm. Hair knotted back; sun had freckled the line where her collar used to hide it. Her eyes held the patience of someone who’d unlearned panic the hard way. She kept one eye on the fountain—the square’s heart—where her children were already soaked.

Jonas walked the cracked marble rim with his arms straight out, chin up, laughing each time he teetered and found his balance. All elbows and certainty, a boy stretching into his shape too fast for his skin. When he smiled, one tooth overlapped, defiant as he was.

Hana hunted him from below, cupping water in both hands and throwing it at his shins, quick as a cat and twice as pleased with herself. Small and quick, still soft in the cheeks; her eyes caught light like glass just pulled from a kiln—hot with curiosity, still forming.

It was a good sound, the kind you memorized without meaning to. Kara let it steady her. She’d learned that some sounds were anchors, and silence was the tide that tried to pull them loose. For a moment she almost believed the sound itself might keep the world from tilting—the laughter of children a spell fragile enough to break if she breathed too hard.

“Morning, Kara!” Marin the baker called, waving her dough-slick fingers. She wore her hair pinned under a kerchief dusted white, and there was flour up to her elbows and the constant flush of oven heat on her cheeks, never fading till evening.

“Morning,” Kara said, and fished a strip of dried fruit from her basket. “Bribe?”

Marin bit it in half and made a show of rolling her eyes skyward. “Saints preserve us. You’ll bankrupt me.” She slipped a round of bread into Kara’s basket—still warm, the crust crackling. “For those bottomless children.”

“They’ll demolish it before we cross the square,” Kara said.

“Good. Let them. Growing is work.” Marin’s smile held, though her eyes had that new Free State look—joy with a string tied to it. Everyone waited for something to pull. The laughter around the ovens felt like borrowed light—bright for now but owed to something darker still waiting to collect.

Kara moved on. Tomas’s hammer set the tempo for this side of the market: ring, ring, hiss of quenching, ring. He was bent over a dented pan, thumb checking the wobble, squinting one eye shut. His forearms were roped and shiny with sweat.

“You’ll beat that poor thing to death,” Kara said.

“Not a chance.” Tomas grinned up at her. “This pan will outlast me.” He reached beneath his bench and produced a tin cup polished bright as a coin. “For Hana. Indestructible.”

“She’ll test the word,” Kara warned, trading him a bundle of thyme and wild mint she’d dried on their windowsill. “Perhaps by this afternoon.”

“I’ll make another by sundown,” he said, like it was nothing. “Children should break things. Means they’re living heavy enough to matter.”

A spice seller—lids sleep-puffy, grin already awake—leaned his elbows on the counter a few stalls down, sleep still in the corners of his eyes and mischief everywhere else. Jars and paper-wrapped cones crowded his space: red, ochre, dirt-brown, ember-black. He said it like a heresy—a truth the Ascendant broadcasts would never permit.

She refused their words for it—‘compliance,’ ‘sequence,’ ‘protocol’—each sounded like metal pressed to skin.

“Kara! Proof of courage,” he announced, pinching crimson powder between two fingers. “Touch and tell me your ancestry.”

“I have no interest in Jonas breathing fire,” she said, but the man had already offered the pinch.

She tapped the barest dust to her tongue and felt heat unspool under her ribs. It dragged a cough out of her and watered her eyes. The spice seller laughed like a bell. The burn painted the air red for her; colour and taste became the same defiance. The heat still shimmered in her mouth as the next aisle opened into colour.

Jonas skidded to her hip with wet feet and the look he always wore when he sensed a dare. “Me.”

“No.” Kara’s hand found his shoulder.

“Just a little.”

“It’s not a game,” she said, but the spice seller was already shaking a few grains into Jonas’s palm. He tossed them in, bravado meeting biology. The boy blinked—coughed himself crooked—and without hesitation, gasping, started to laugh through the tears. The seller thumped his back, delighted. Relief came with his laughter—sharp and thin, like glass catching sun. She smiled.

“Brave or foolish?” Kara asked when Jonas could breathe again.

“Both,” he said, and wiped his eyes with the heel of his hand, wholly unrepentant.

The cloth merchant’s booth hung with uneven banners of colour: faded rose, pond green, a blue so worn it had become its own sky. The merchant’s fingers were stained with dye; his smile arrived a heartbeat before his sales pitch. Laughter was not a crime in the Free States, but some habits died slowly.

On the edge of her vision, a flicker on a cracked screen—an old social post still looping. A smiling child, maybe six, captioned: 'First ration cake!' The tower that hosted it was scrap now. The Ash Rats had tagged this sector weeks ago—a streak of ash, a curve that broke halfway through. Even in shade the soot mark caught stray light and swallowed it.

If they’d come and gone, there’d be no batteries left. She checked anyway. A mural, sun-worn but defiant, showed a spiral open hand—the Free States sign for shared thought. Beside it, a rusted antenna spiked from the rubble like a church steeple.

The mark echoed the old Ember Coil—a spiral flame held in open hands.

Someone had painted an old AI glyph beneath it—an eye inside a circuit. When the sun moved, its lines caught red for a breath and vanished again. A mural, newer than the rest, was stencilled in crimson: 'Freedom is messy—that's why it's worth it.'

“For your little one,” he said, lifting a scarf the green of worn bottle glass. “See? It will pull the sea into her eyes.”

Hana arrived dripping, drawn by treasure-magnetism. “Please, Mama.”

“It’s too fine,” Kara said. “You’ll ruin it.”

“It will mean she lived in it,” the merchant said gently.

He set the scarf on Hana’s shoulders as if it weighed the same as blessing. “Colour is meant to work.”

Hana clasped her hands in exaggerated supplication. “Please, please, please.”

Kara felt the give in herself and sighed. She traded a small pouch of beans. Hana twirled until the scarf lifted and settled again, her laughter fluttering like cloth in the wind.

“Looks like a flag,” Jonas declared. “I should have one.”

“You’d lose it before the next stall.”

“Flags don’t need to last. They just need to fly,” he said.

From the edge of the square, Farid lifted one hand, calling her with two fingers like he always had. He sat behind his table of oddments, his beard more white than black now, his knuckles swollen and stubborn. Wires. Beads. Bottle caps. A buckled watch strap. He held up a piece of glass the size of a thumbnail, rounded and threaded on a loop of wire.

“Found up north,” he said. “It was a window in a life we didn’t live.” His tone went soft. “This is the piece that remembered how to be beautiful.”

“She’ll lose it,” Kara said, because she always did. It had become ritual,

“Someone will find it and make a story,” Farid said. “Nothing is ever truly lost. Not here.”

The words settled in her chest like a small weight that made her stand straighter.

Kara traded him six coffee beans, precious as coins. He sniffed them, pleased, and pressed the glass into Hana’s damp palm. When the sun caught it, she saw herself in miniature—blurred, doubled, held inside the colour. ”A jewel for a queen.”

Hana’s face went reverent. She showed Jonas swiftly, and he pretended not to be impressed.

A musician had found a corner between two load-bearing walls and set a beat with his palms on a barrel drum. Someone else shook a string of metal washers threaded on wire and made it sound like a lazy rain.

Snatches of words drifted past, catching on Kara as she walked. “—council promised more beans this rotation.” “I heard from a runner—” “—lies to make us nervous—” “—if they ever come, we’ll meet them with fire—” “—my sister says they put something on your head—” “—hush, not in front of the children—”

Kara pretended the words were weather. She had learned the Order way of not hearing and then unlearned it one market day at a time. She adjusted the bread in her basket so the loaf could breathe and checked the fountain again. Jonas had found a stick and was pretending it was a spear; Hana tried to knock him from the rim with splashes timed to his steps. He let himself fall once, to hear her victorious shriek.

The beekeeper had set up on a shaded table with jars lined like amber glass. A soft hum emanated from the wicker crates beneath. “For your girl?” the woman asked.

“Not today,” Kara said, and hated the way disappointment flickered across Hana’s face and quickly hid. “Next time.”

“Next time,” the beekeeper echoed.

At a low table, a cobbler in a leather apron cut sandals from tire treads. His knife left black curls on the ground. “Look,” Jonas said, squatting to watch. “They’d grip better than mine.”

“Bring me your feet,” the cobbler invited. He pressed Jonas’s heel to a strip, measured with a length of twine, nodded solemnly. “You run? You look like a runner.”

“I run,” Jonas said, as if it were already a career.

“Come back with a promise and a little leather,” the cobbler said. “I’ll make you feel like a mountain goat.”

The mechanic’s stall was a map of old-world ghosts: wrenches big as forearms, pulleys, gears with teeth worn smooth from use. Between metal and memory, the air smelled faintly of ozone.

A hand-lettered sign read Ask before touching. The mechanic—thin as a drawn line, with grease up his wrists—picked up a long-handled wrench when he saw Jonas but did not hand it over. “This turned bolts on machines taller than your house,” he said. “We used to lift bridges with cousins of this.”

“What do we lift now?” Jonas asked.

“Each other,” the mechanic said without irony. He let Jonas hold the wrench without hesitation, one hand on the haft with him, sharing the weight. “Heavy, see? Strength without aim is only a dropped tool.”

Kara thanked him with a nod she hoped carried the gratitude she didn’t have words for. She was tired of telling Jonas no and grateful for the men and women in the market who could tell him how. Watching him listen, she felt the faint ache of pride.

Two women near the herb seller argued—one with a frayed braid, one flashing a gold tooth when she bit her words.

“The council should ask the northern States for help.”

“And invite their trouble with it?”

“We’re already in it, Lysa.”

“Lower your tone. Children, for mercy’s sake—”

A guard walked by with the lazy authority of peacetime, a rifle slung, a mug of tea in his hand, boots unlaced one eyelet, tea steam fogging his lip. Kara recognized his profile; she’d seen him on the wall a dozen times but never learned his name. He tipped his mug to a vendor he knew.

The fountain held the square together. It had been here before the war; its inscriptions were worn illegible except for the sea-curve of a few letters. Somebody cleaned it every morning, scrubbing moss from the cracks and clearing leaves from the pump. Children’s chalk drawings ringed its base—flowers with too many petals, suns with too many rays, names written in colours that had to be bartered for one brilliant stub at a time. The water wasn’t plentiful, but it was clear and cold, and to hear it run was to remember that the world hadn’t given up its gifts completely.

Kara set her basket on the rim and dipped her fingers. The chill crawled up her wrist. Hana came at once, pressing her wet head to Kara’s waist, the new scarf a green ribbon on the air. In the Order’s cities, even a shard of coloured glass like this might once have been enough to invite questions.

“Do you like it?” she asked, tipping her face up, water beading on her lashes.

“I love it,” Kara said, and meant I love you in it; I love you like this.

Jonas made a last triumphant lap of the rim and hopped down. “Did you see the wrench?” he asked without taking a breath. “I could have lifted—”

“You could have dislocated your shoulder,” Kara said, automatic, and, softened. “Tell me about it on the way home.”

“Can we take the long way?” Hana asked. “By the wall. I want to count the pigeons.” She remembered games children used to play under the Order—trial games, silence games, little rehearsals for fear. She had stopped asking questions early.

“The long way wastes bread,” Kara said. “But yes. If we move before Jonas eats the entire loaf with his eyes.”

He leaned in and exaggeratedly inhaled the bread’s steam, foolish and dear. Marin across the way saw him and made a show of swatting him in the air with her towel. He dodged the invisible blow and bowed.

Kara let the moment rest on her palms like the weight of the tin cup: small, cool, useful. She’d learned to carry more than one thing at once: hunger, laughter, dread, errands. The secret here was not the absence of dread, but the work of living anyway.

“Can we get honey next time?” Hana asked, tone small in the way it got when she was trying not to want. She turned the glass bead over in her palm like a star.

“Next time,” Kara promised. She didn’t promise more than that. She had learned the cost of larger vows.

A dog nosed past with the confident shuffle of a professional scrounger, and a teenage boy shooed it from the meat stall with a rag he’d meant to be stern and couldn’t quite make. Someone whistled a fragment of a song and couldn’t find the end of it, so the drummer gave him one. The laundress blew steam off a kettle and poured it back and forth between two cups to cool, making fog.

“Ma,” Jonas said, his tone shifting from playful to serious in an instant. “If I were on the wall, do you think I could—”

“No,” Kara said, and without hesitation, because it was already too late for single words, “not yet.”

“When?” he asked.

“When your arms fill out and your tone stops deciding every hour what it wants to be,” she said, and smiled to soften it.

“I’m nearly taller than you.”

“You’ve been nearly taller than me for a year.”

Hana tugged her scarf up like armour and looked toward the wall. “I want to be a scientist. Like Zhou.” She frowned. “Or a beekeeper. Bees listen better.”

“Bees don’t listen at all,” Jonas said. “They agree in a group and pretend it was ideas.”

“Like you,” Hana said, swift as a peck.

Kara spread a palm between them—a quiet signal, the one that meant I love this; keep it from drawing blood.

The market went on being itself. A woman bartered three needles for a comb. A man held a bent nail between his teeth and repaired a basket handle with new cane. A child held out a hand to the drummer; the drummer tapped a rhythm on the tiny palm and made the boy feel like he had an instrument. Two men at the far edge counted beans into piles and nodded after each ten like they were answering a question only they could hear.

Kara traced the seam in the fountain’s stone where some long-ago break had been mended with a plate of bolted steel. The repair was crude and perfect and exactly like the life that had grown around it: good enough to hold, proof that you didn’t need the old world’s polish to have function, to have grace.

She traced it once more, the warmth of the metal faint under her fingers, as if the world itself still held a pulse.

“Ready?” she asked her children.

“Five more splashes,” Hana bargained, holding up a hand with all the fingers. She miscounted and made six. Jonas smirked and said nothing. Somewhere in the Order’s bastions, this hour would belong to the Unity Broadcast.

Kara nodded. “Five. And no pushing.”

“No hard pushing,” Jonas amended.

Kara almost told him not to be clever. Instead, she leaned her hip against the stone and let the water sound do what water always did—make time feel like it was moving in the right direction.

She watched, memorising the moment as if she could fold it and keep it in a pocket: Marin’s floury smile; Tomas’s bright cup; the scarf green as river glass; Farid’s careful hands; the drummer’s steady wrists; Jonas crowing and Hana’s feet flashing and the fountain holding.

What she didn’t know was that the picture she kept would soon be all that remained of it. Beyond the hills, engines stirred—the opening note of the silence to come, quiet enough that the market’s breath almost covered it.
