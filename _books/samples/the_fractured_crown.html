Sample — Opening Chapter

A City Without Shadows

Light over the capital didn’t fall; it arrived.

It came with the precision of code—white-gold over terraces, transit lines, and stone that had learned its reflection. The city rose to meet it, shadowless. Windows answered in collective gleam; towers along the administrative spine unfurled panels as if the day were decree.

No clocks were needed. The light itself was the hour.

From speakers hidden in the city’s seams, a voice of no one threaded the streets. Serenity is service. Harmony sustains. The words asked nothing; belief had already been supplied, like water and rationed bread. People stepped from doorways, faces quiet. Their greetings echoed the civic voice—accurate, unburdened, low. The Wheel of Obedience repeated along façades—twelve spokes balanced to dizziness. A passerby might glance, register perfection, and feel a pressure ease at the base of the skull.

In a civic classroom near the ministry quarter, twenty children bent over slates. Each stylus moved in time with the wall’s quiet pulse. They copied the morning phrase—Serenity is service—one measured stroke at a time. The teacher’s voice held the same cadence—no faster, no slower. When a child faltered on a curve, the desk light dimmed until she resumed. No reprimand followed; none was needed. The room seemed to breathe with her correction, exhaling once she was back in rhythm.

For half a second the wall’s pulse missed its beat; no one seemed to notice.

The children read the line together. The sound was even, without rise or fall, a tide of small voices that left no echo when it withdrew.

Transit drifted on magnetic veins—motion without friction, distance denied its hush. A low vibration pulsed through the paving, faint enough to be civic heartbeat, too steady to notice. Above the ordered lanes, drones patrolled on fixed vectors, their rotors tuned to the city’s own frequency. They cast no shadow; the light accommodated them.

At the junction of two transit spines, maintenance workers waited. A train glided past on magnetized pads. The air trembled faintly from the current; even vibration seemed to follow regulation. One worker spoke too quickly into her comm—a pulse of real urgency clipping the morning’s smoothness. Another touched her sleeve in correction, not warning—just alignment.

They stayed silent until the civic voice resumed from the overhead grid.

When they spoke again, their voices were indistinguishable, tone corrected to civic neutrality. The train slid away without a sound that could be called arrival or departure.

On side streets, markets arranged themselves with the grammar the Order preferred: fruit (when there was fruit) in gradients from green to red; jars in ranks by weight and clarity; tools by life cycle. A faint trellis of transmission shimmered overhead, making visible the Tempath Glyph in its public form—rings folding through one another like breath. Children traced it with their hands until a parent lowered a palm and the mimicry stilled.

Those who served the centres went to them; those in the margins cleaned them.

Drones filled what space remained.

The city offered only the necessary murmur—footsteps on swept stone, elevator air whispering between floors, the civic hymn with no beginning or end. The gleam never shifted: the first ten minutes after dawn, indefinitely prolonged—a moment held until it forgot itself.

On a corner near the Directorate’s silver-blue glass, a public screen cycled through its morning feed: weather icons in even lines, currency equivalence, a five-frame instruction on safety near municipal dig sites. Then the Eye—metallic iris, data rings etched in orbit—hovered a beat longer than any image. Beneath it ran the caption that replaced the old civic prayer: Knowledge is certainty. No one paused; no one hurried past. It was part of the city’s contour—ordinary as a curb.

Beyond the capital, smaller towns mimicked the same choreography. Light reached them too—not the full white-gold, only what weather allowed. Broadcast spines along the ridgelines turned together. A maintenance crew rode to a pylon, gloved hands smoothing its casing as if a bedspread’s crease. Even the wastelands, when the signal reached them, looked briefly tempered metal cooled under oil. In deeper, shielded places, a few pockets of silence persisted absence of tone only; the notes were written regardless.

On a side street below the ministry towers, a man paused at a vending stall beneath a banner reading Harmony Feeds All. The civic voice recited the hour’s refrain—Service is freedom. He mouthed the words as everyone did—automatic, practiced. Freedom snagged in his throat; what escaped was a breath that almost said forgive.

The stall’s light faltered. The vendor looked up, silent. A tone issued from the curb speaker—soft, corrective, almost kind. He repeated the phrase, voice level now. The light steadied. He stepped back into pedestrian flow, posture unbroken, mouth closed. No one turned to watch him go.

At the administrative core, elevators rose and fell at speeds calibrated to feel polite. Doors opened. Doors closed. Reports passed from hand to hand, screen to screen, as light through clean glass. No one hurried. No one slowed. Every gesture had the patience of process.

In that deliberate morning and posture of ease, one detail kept returning—too subtle for warning, too constant for chance.

Where the Wheel was carved above Meridian Plaza and the glyph revolved, a softness lived at one spoke—a ring not quite in tune with the rest. Not a crack. Not even error. The smallest hesitation of light.

The city did not hesitate.

Elsewhere—beneath that same obedient light—the world narrowed to one room and the woman who refused to vanish inside it.

Beneath the Ascendant grid, light changed its mind about what it meant to illuminate.

Elevators sank through sealed shafts, each level marked not by name but by tone.

The descent’s hum deepened. It stopped pretending to be mechanical and began to resemble breath.

Facility Nine announced itself by temperature—a degree warmer than the capital, as if the structure generated its own weather. Doors read clearance by pulse.

Walls adjusted brightness to motion, never shadowing a human figure enough to grant privacy.

Zhou Virell moved through the lower corridor, a wafer pressed to her palm. Her face had the careful sharpness of someone who had traded sleep for certainty—eyes overlit by screens, mouth trained neat and flat.

At each intersection a panel blinked awake, reading her vitals before permitting the next turn.

Technicians she passed kept their eyes lowered—not reverence, habit; the building recorded every glance.

A notice crawled across a wall in thin white type:

Sector Black – Lattice Interface Testing in Progress.

The text erased itself mid-sentence, swallowed by its own discretion.

Zhou paused beside an observation port. Beyond the glass, coolant mist coiled around data-conduit towers, each throbbing with the same rhythm that governed the capital above. If she stood long enough, she felt the pulse climb her bones. It was supposed to be reassuring.

A junior technician emerged from a side door, carrying a tray of labelled syringes. He froze when he saw her, posture snapping too formal.

“Director Virell.”

Zhou’s gaze flicked to the tray, then back to his face. “Delivery for Sector-Black?”

“Yes, Director.” His grip tightened slightly, the tray rattling once before he steadied it.

“Then move,” she said—neither harsh nor kind.

He nodded, stepping aside.

When his footsteps dissolved into the hum, she exhaled once—quiet, efficient—and continued toward the holding wing.

Facility Nine was built to erase corners; walls curved inward, guiding movement forward.

Even standing still felt like disobedience.

Security glass divided each sector; in the right light, reflections of passing staff merged with silhouettes inside—order and subject for a moment the same image before parting again.

As she walked, a door on her left whispered open. A woman sat inside, eyes unfocused, lips moving in rhythm with the distant pulse. The wall beside her displayed one line: Alignment under review.

Alignment under review.

The door closed.

Zhou tapped her wafer at the final checkpoint.

A voice descended from the ceiling—neither male nor female yet carrying authority.

“Director Virell. Clearance granted. Subject K-TR/187 prepared for evaluation.”

The word subject brushed against something she refused to name.

She straightened her coat and stepped through the sterilisation gate.

Beyond it, the corridor tightened.

White light pressed close from all directions until space itself seemed to hum.

Ahead waited Kara’s door—seamless save for the indent where a hand might touch, not to open but to be recognised.

Zhou set her palm to the panel.

The surface warmed, confirming identity.

Inside, light rose by a measurable fraction.

It was time to begin.

Far below ground level, light held the same white-gold tone, though its source was a gridded ceiling set in polished composite. Walls pale, floor sheened like a surface that had never known grit. Handleless doors aligned so precisely the hall felt smaller than it was. Each doorplate bore the Wheel of Obedience, a number beneath it meaning more to the building than to the people inside.

Behind one door, Kara sat in a room arranged to look like choice. Her hair, once sun-browned from market days in Dovai, lay in a blunt, regulation cut that couldn’t quite hide the silver gathering at her temples. Lines had settled at the corners of her eyes—not age so much as the record of years spent refusing to fold.

The bed smelled of ozone and pressed citrus. Shelves set flush in the wall held three books with blank spines and a vase with a single green branch that might have been alive. A low table held a slate teapot and two cups, white as the light. Breakfast had come earlier, precise to the minute, the gesture more procedure than care: a small loaf glazed and warm at centre; a bowl of pulse with cumin and lemon; preserved pear; water cold enough to show its own clarity. The tray hadn’t arrived through a slot but with a nod—hospitality as policy.

She ate because refusing would be written down as a kind of noise.

When she finished, the second cup remained untouched.

The room’s screen woke unbidden, showing the capital above. Streets in their clean patience, faces in quiet, drones moving on fixed vectors that might as well be lines on the air. The Veiled Eye blinked once in a civic cycle and moved on. Kara looked, then blacked the screen. Nothing in the room belonged to her—only the order of looking.

She set her palm to the wall. The building’s hum met her palm through the composite—a restrained heartbeat that never admitted to being one. She counted breaths, then stopped counting when the counting began to feel like permission.

Memory asked for its time.

Not the market, not that day of cumin smoke and blistered dough. The activation had found her here—Facility Nine, Sector-Black—after Dovai. That city of heat and barter belonged to the world before. After the fall, the Order brought her and the children into an architecture that never used the word take. In the first week the hum stayed distant; in the second it moved closer, threading the air until the air was mostly thread.

Doors learned her steps, widening a fraction before she reached them. The Choir’s armour made geometry of corridors. Sometimes, eyes closed, she felt sound align her ribs like a brace fitted in sleep.

She pressed fingers to the ridge of her wrist.

Named what would not be taken.

Hana. Jonas.

A chime sounded—gentle, domestic—and the door thinned open.

Two Black Choir guards entered first—matte charcoal, helmets glassed where eyes should have been, the fine chain of their gloves catching light like patient rain. Between them walked Zhou Virell in the white of laboratory clergy, wafer folded in one hand.

Kara’s throat tried to shape a name it no longer owned, but the sound folded back into breath.

“Kara,” Zhou said—not Subject, not code. Her voice found the register it once used with her—careful, exact.

“You don’t know what that word means anymore,” Kara said.

Zhou let a breath pass. “We’re needed in Observation Bay Nine.”

“For what?”

“For a protocol read—genetic therapy trial. We’re removing the resistance markers; you and the others will be monitored during exposure.”

“The other captives,” Kara said.

“The other participants,” Zhou corrected softly, as if the difference lived in a vowel.

Kara rose. “Where are my children?”

“In their designated quarters. Instruction first cycle. After the session, I’ll request a visit.”

“Request,” Kara echoed.

Zhou didn’t look away. “The request will carry my name.”

Kara stepped to the threshold. The technician who had brought breakfast stood in the corner, still as furniture feigning purpose.

The corridor stretched in designed calm, its hum subtly matching their steps.

“You’ve always liked lemon,” Zhou said, as if they walked a city window instead of armoured wall. “I asked the kitchen to add it.”

Kara kept her eyes forward. “You remember flavours, not names.”

“Names complicate logistics.” Zhou smiled—small, automatic.

“They make people human.”

“Humans complicate order.”

They passed a junction where the ceiling dipped to clear a conduit. Zhou slowed until they walked side by side—an old habit from when conversation meant collaboration, not risk.

“When did you stop asking why?” Kara asked.

Zhou tilted her head. “When asking stopped changing the answer.”

“Or when it started costing you comfort.”

The guard on Kara’s left shifted, armour rasping. Zhou did not look his way; she folded her hands behind her back like a teacher choosing patience. “You speak as if the world was stolen. It was corrected.”

Kara laughed—short, without amusement. “You always were better at syntax than truth.”

They passed an intersection marked by a glyph that pulsed with their speech. Zhou’s tone softened, almost human again. “I still have truth, Kara—it just fits the data better now.”

“The data.” Kara’s voice lowered, edge sharper. “What about people?”

“People are what the data is for,” Zhou said after a pause. “Including you.” The data hummed louder than belief.

“That’s supposed to make me grateful?”

“It’s supposed to keep you alive.”

Silence settled, filled only by the Choir’s steps and the air’s soft modulation. The corridor bent; the floor pattern repeated every seven tiles—a rhythm Kara already knew.

Zhou spoke again, quieter. “Do you ever think of before—the lab in Dovai, the day you called the lattice a myth?”

“I think about the moment you stopped agreeing with me.”

Zhou pressed her lips thin. “It was never agreement. It was doubt. Doubt must be contained or it spreads.”

“Containment,” Kara said, letting the word hang like smoke.

Ahead, a door panel lit, signalling the approaching lift. The light flickered once, catching their faces in shared reflection.

Kara broke the silence. “If this place fixes the world, why does it still sound like something dying?”

Zhou looked straight ahead. “Systems hum when they stabilise.”

“They hum when they’re hungry.”

The guards stood still. The vibration thickened, as if the facility disapproved of excess emotion.

They walked the rest of the way in silence, but it had changed character—it felt inhabited.

A lift opened before they arrived. Inside, light deepened to amber at the edges—an engineered sunrise. As doors sealed, pressure shifted—the floor giving that small surrender Kara had learned to hate for its politeness.

Zhou checked the wafer. “We’re trialling a sequence predicted to attenuate the K-series variance.”

“And if it works?” Kara asked.

“You’ll continue receiving care,” Zhou said, hands steady. “The world becomes simpler.” Her voice didn’t quite fit the air.

“Simple isn’t a virtue,” Kara said. “It’s a trimming.”

The lift paused; the doors stayed closed.

“I want to see Hana and Jonas—now.”

Zhou paused—the first time—then shook her head. “After.”

Kara moved—stepping between guards and doors, angling for the sensor. One Choir guard reached—chain bright, movement quick. She turned her shoulder to slip past, every muscle remembering other corridors, other men. The second caught her upper arm; the first pinned her forearm—precision over strength. Pain sharpened the lift’s air.

Kara hissed between teeth she meant to keep for speaking. “I’ll see them.”

“Restrain,” one guard said, adjusting grip.

Zhou’s voice cut through the hum. “She isn’t resisting protocol—she’s remembering.”

The guard held. “Directive is containment.”

“Directive is observation,” Zhou said, colder now. “You overstep.”

The grip held one beat too long before easing.

Kara’s breath came uneven, a tremor she refused to name. “You’re all the same,” she said. “You hide behind rules and call it mercy.”

Zhou’s gaze flicked to her—an old reflex, almost pity, almost warning. “Hold,” she said, sharper. “Enough.” Then, lower: “I remember. Don’t give them reason to forget who you are.”

Kara stilled. She didn’t lower her chin.

The lift released; doors opened onto a corridor that had forgotten colour. Grey so pale it seemed transparent, panels breathing their seam-light in time with machinery below.

They passed glassed bays—narrow rooms lit by vertical beams. In each, someone sat tilted a few degrees toward the ceiling—listening for a sound that might be the building or themselves. A woman whose hands still remembered healing, a boy no older than Jonas, mouth slack in practiced calm. Their chests rose and fell with machine regularity. None turned.

Zhou caught Kara’s look. “Baseline subjects,” she said. “The treatment starts with those already aligned; their readings model variance before exposure.” Her tone stayed clinical, a scientist reciting weather.

Kara watched them. “Aligned,” she said. “So, obedience earns a chair instead of a chain.”

Zhou’s eyes flicked over. “They volunteered. The Order rewards devotion.”

“By locking them in a room and calling it purpose,” Kara said. “You imprison the loyal and the disobedient both—only the name on the door changes.”

Zhou kept her eyes forward. The hum folded over their footsteps until speech felt redundant.

The descent through Facility Nine reached the heart of Sector Black. Heat gathered; a low vibration ran the floor; ribbed shielding replaced observation glass. The air tasted of metal and a sweetness that never belonged to flowers.

At an antechamber, the floor showed concentric rings—conduits glinting under resin. At centre waited a circular door etched with the Tempath Glyph, its motion constant and almost imperceptible.

“Observation Bay,” Zhou said, touching the wafer to a panel. The rings brightened and parted.

Inside, the chamber widened into white light. Translucent floors showed machinery churning below—motion like veins beneath skin. A central column rose through the space, glass wound with copper filaments. Light inside moved like trapped weather. Around it, consoles formed a half-circle, each manned by a white-clad figure in rehearsed posture.

Zhou gestured. “Here.”

Kara stepped onto a low platform marked by pale circles. The floor recognised her weight; a vibration climbed from feet to spine.

“Begin primary variance read.”

One technician keyed the sequence, another confirmed. The column brightened; a hum rose from the walls, finding the pitch of bone.

A band of warmth slid down Kara’s chest, settling just above her heart.

Almost like touch—steady, deliberate, impersonal.

The hum thickened, melodic enough to resemble a choir—vowels without language.

“Stability baseline steady,” a voice reported.

“Introduce vector A, point-one,” Zhou said.

Kara felt the warmth change pitch, as if light leaned closer to listen. Behind her eyelids a thread of viridian crossed the gold—gone before she could be sure it had existed.

Zhou looked to a console. “Marker present.”

Kara heard. “You see it.”

“I see what the system shows me,” Zhou said evenly.

“And what do you call it?”

Zhou did not look up. “Interference.”

Kara smiled without humour. “That’s what you call a heartbeat.”

Zhou’s hands moved faster. “Repeat.”

“Response minimal,” a technician echoed.

Zhou again: “Repeat.”

The hum climbed; light tightened around Kara’s chest until it felt almost like hands.

“Variance unchanged,” another voice reported.

“Again,” Zhou said, sharper. “Increase vector A point-one.”

Warmth pressed inward, air turning sweet with ozone. Kara’s pulse quickened, but she refused to move. The therapy wanted compliance; she gave it none.

Images rose: Hana stacking stones; Jonas laughing through fear because naming it changed its shape. The hum swallowed the sound of it, folding memory into harmony.

“If this works—” Zhou began, then stopped. “Again.”

Pain narrowed to precision. Kara drew a slow breath and let it come.

Zhou checked the readout, saw the tremor, and stilled her hands. “Hold.” Her voice softened. “Record and clear sequence.”

She exhaled; eyes fixed on the data. “We’re close—just need cleaner response curves next cycle.”

Kara’s voice came quiet, blade sharp. “Say we again and tell me who it is.”

Zhou exhaled—the same sound she once used to steady herself in another life. “The Order,” she said. “The world. The part of me that still believes it’s kinder to remove the fight than to demand it.”

“From me,” Kara said. “Or from my children.”

Zhou’s gaze lifted, unshielded and exact. “From everyone.”

The band of warmth lifted; radiance steadied in the column. The voice declared, “Read complete. Log and hold.”

Kara stepped back. For a moment her knees forgot how to belong to her, as if the field still claimed the space inside her bones. The platform’s vibration followed her ankles—a quiet pulse trying to remember itself.

The Choir reformed around her in silent symmetry. Their movement felt like mathematics—an equation solving for obedience. Zhou powered down the wafer; its surface dimmed but refused to fold—light trapped between orders.

Beyond the chamber, air cooled back toward doctrine—the hum in the walls resumed its polite monotone. At the lift, a technician waited—empty tray balanced like an offering. He looked at neither woman, only at the space beyond—as if waiting for a cue that never came.

Kara frowned. “Since when do they send attendants between sectors?”

Zhou hesitated—long enough for the pause to mean something. “Protocol changes,” she said. “Assignments rotate now.”

Kara studied her. “Or watching from closer range.”

Zhou glanced at the guards, then the ceiling—as if confirmation still came from above—and nodded once. “Southern corridor,” she said, tone mild enough to hide direction.

They moved. The route curved along glass revealing only lighted shapes—rooms outlined but withholding detail. The air thickened, as if it too had been instructed not to speak.

Beyond, another door stayed closed.

“Authorization,” one guard said.

Zhou lifted the wafer. The panel blinked once—denied access.

“Access restricted,” said the other guard, voice machine flat. “Command clearance only.” Since the uprisings near Grid Node 07, upper sectors locked their own doors.

Zhou’s glance cut to the readout. “There shouldn’t be restrictions on this level.” Her tone stayed measured, though something behind it tightened.

Kara studied her. “Problem?”

“Routine lockdown,” Zhou said too fast, turning to the guards. “We’ll reroute.”

Kara caught the tone before the words. “You’re lying.”

The guards repositioned; one gestured for Kara to step back. When she didn’t, a hand caught her arm—firm, not cruel. The reflex came anyway—a cold clarity, the body remembering other captures.

“Enough,” Zhou said, sharper now. “She’s not a threat.”

The grip eased but did not release. The panel stayed silent; the door refused acknowledgment.

Zhou exhaled through her nose, regaining formality. “Return her to quarters.” Then, lower—almost human: “Don’t make them question my clearance.”

She almost said Kara’s name as she once did—then remembered the cameras.

Kara met her gaze. “Then stop pretending you still have any.”

Zhou didn’t answer. The guards shifted armour rasping faintly, the only sign they’d heard.

The lift took them upward. Descent and ascent were the same hum—the same temperature and courtesy, a machine refusing to admit to movement. The corridor’s hum thickened, swallowing the words before they could echo.

Back in the residence wing no one called a detention floor, doors lay in identical sequence. Yet Kara felt each one as a heartbeat she could count. At her door the guards stepped aside. Zhou halted where the threshold turned the space into a frame.

“You did well,” Zhou said.

“I didn’t help you,” Kara replied.

“That isn’t what I said.”

Kara stood at the border. “If anything happens to them—”

Zhou’s tone stayed even. “It won’t.” She believed it the way a scientist believes an outcome that should hold if every variable behaves.

She lingered longer than protocol allowed. “Eat,” she said at last; here, care had to sound like instruction. “Rest. I’ll return next cycle.”

Kara crossed the line. The door sealed with a soft exhale, the room receiving her like a perfected habit.

She set both palms to the window strip—a rectangle of treated glass showing corridor and the illusion of light behaving. The wall’s resonance carried a faint shift—not quite discord, not yet order—passing through her like breath finding its own pace.

Between one moment and the next, the radiance remembered itself—on the glass, her handprint lingered a heartbeat longer than physics allowed—then smoothed clean.

The console on the far wall woke unbidden. Its surface glowed low and undecorated until an image resolved: two figures at a white table, heads bent over identical slates. Hana’s hair was longer; Jonas’s shoulders had found shape. They looked well—or curated to. Kara stepped closer, knees suddenly light, her palm leaving a faint heat-print on the glass before she realised she had reached for it. The feed carried no sound, no caption—only the stillness of observation.

She didn’t know who had sent it or what belief it was meant to stir. The image flickered once, twice, then vanished—screen dark, hum unchanged.

Her reflection surfaced in the black glass—eyes bright, mouth half open—as if the screen had borrowed her face and refused to give it back.

“Hana,” she said into the quiet. “Jonas.”

Facility Nine listened in its way—and, as always, said nothing back.

Only the faint imprint of her hand remained, cooling on the glass—proof that something warm had existed.
